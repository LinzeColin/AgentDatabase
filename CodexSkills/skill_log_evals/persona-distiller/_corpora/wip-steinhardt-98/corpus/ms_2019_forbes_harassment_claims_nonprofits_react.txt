After Harassment Claims Against Billionaire Michael Steinhardt, Nonprofits React In Very Different Ways

Michael Steinhardt, one of Wall Street’s first hedge fund traders, has been accused of sexual harassment and inappropriate behavior by six women, the New York Times and Pro Publica reported Thursday morning. The nonprofits and institutions to which he donated generously are split on the reaction to the allegations.
Some of the allegations include claims that Steinhardt propositioned women and made lewd, sexually explicit comments to them during meetings about donating to philanthropic causes. Steinhardt , who Forbes estimates is worth $1.1 billion, admits to “boorish, disrespectful and just plain dumb” behavior, but he denies harassment.
Steinhardt, 78, has donated at least $20 million to Birthright Israel, a nonprofit he cofounded with fellow Jewish billionaire Charles Bronfman in 1999 that has sent more than 600,000 Jews on free trips to Israel. According to the Times, Birthright’s director of communications from 2001 to 2010 alleges that Steinhardt asked if she would have a threesome with him and one of her female colleagues during a reception in Jerusalem. A spokesman for Steinhardt told the New York Times that the accusation was “simply not true.”
In a statement, Birthright Israel says the organization is not aware of any inappropriate behavior by Steinhardt, who is an honorary board member. “To the best of our knowledge, we do not have any record of a complaint made by the former employee noted against Michael Steinhardt,” the Birthright spokesperson said.
Steinhardt, who turned to philanthropy after making his fortune on Wall Street, splashed his money around many of New York City’s cultural and educational institutions, including the Brooklyn Botanical Gardens and the Metropolitan Museum of Art, where the Steinhardt gallery features Greek works. Steinhardt’s foundations have donated at least $127 million to nonprofits since 2003, the Times reports.
Steinhardt also has a school named after him: New York University’s Steinhardt School, which offers graduate and undergraduate programs in art, education, music, health and other areas. John Beckman, an NYU spokesman, said via email that Steinhardt, who is a trustee at NYU, has been a “generous” supporter of the private university for many years. “However, the incidents recounted in today's New York Times article are troubling and out-of-step with appropriate standards of conduct. The University will be initiating a review into Mr. Steinhardt's interactions with NYU students, faculty, and staff,” Beckman added.
WisdomTree Investments, an exchange-traded fund shop where Steinhardt is chairman of the board, said it is “closely monitoring the news relating to Michael Steinhardt.” “We have policies in place focused on ensuring a safe, respectful work environment. We continue to evaluate this matter and will take appropriate actions as we see fit,” the company said.
The Steinhardt Foundation for Jewish Life, which Steinhardt started in 1994 and remains the chairman of the board, says in a statement that the Times led “a six-month campaign” with the goal to “dig up dirt on Michael.” The foundation admitted that Steinhardt is known for “provocative” jokes, but added that Steinhardt never intended his “inappropriate banter” to be taken as anything other than jests.
“Admittedly, Michael’s sense of humor can be insensitive, and he has apologized for the unintended bad feelings his remarks have caused. As he said months ago: ‘I am sorry and deeply regret causing any embarrassment, discomfort or pain which was never my intention,’” the statement reads.
The statement, signed by four executives of the Steinhardt Foundation, describes his behavior as lighthearted: “Michael is an equal-opportunity teaser—teasing men and women alike. The reporters apparently overlooked this fact because teasing men and women alike doesn’t make for a great story.”
Steinhardt is a well-known Wall Street figure. Born in Bensonhurst, Brooklyn, he started one of the first dozen or so hedge funds in New York in the 1960s with about $8 million under management and grew it to around $5 billion in the 1990s. According to a Forbes cover story from 2014, Steinhardt Partners achieved a 24.5% average annual return from 1967 to 1995, the year he closed the hedge fund operation.
In his autobiography, No Bull: My Life In and Out of Markets , the billionaire admits that he was an aggressive and harsh boss and that his employees “used expressions like 'battered children,' 'mental abuse,' 'random violence' and 'rage disorder'” while describing what it was like to work at Steinhardt Partners.
The Hillel International, a Jewish nonprofit, has distanced itself from Steinhardt, as the New York Times reported. After conducting an investigation into Steinhardt’s alleged behavior against at least two of Hillel’s employees, the nonprofit removed him from its board of governors and chose not to solicit a $50,000 donation he recently pledged. In one of the alleged incidents, a female executive said that Steinhardt asked her to have sex with him multiple times during a meeting about donations.
In January, Tina Price, the chair of Hillel International’s board of directors, and Eric D. Fingerhut, president and CEO, sent a memo to Hillel employees regarding its investigation into sexual harassment claims against donors. (The memo does not specifically identify Steinhardt.)
“Hillel International has recently completed investigations into claims by staff members of sexual harassment by donors. In each case, it was found that the complaints were justified, and that the individuals had been subjected to inappropriate comments and/or suggestions by one or more of our donors,” the memo, which Forbes obtained, read. “In one case, investigators found that Hillel’s response was not timely, thereby causing additional harm. It pains us greatly that anyone in the Hillel movement could be subjected to any form of harassment. We have apologized to these respected professionals.”
