THE CONSTRUCTION OF THE THAMES TUNNEL 


15. BRUNEL, Sir Marc I. Inventor and Engineer; constructed the Thames 
Tunnel [1825—43]. ALS, two full pages, 4to, July 20, 1829. Fine. | 
$125.00 


An outstanding letter on the early problems of constructing a tunnel under the 
Thames River in England. Panics and strikes took place among the workmen, and in 
1828, after the second irruption of the river occurred, the tunnel was bricked up for 
seven years. “I feel much obliged for a notice that has appeared before the public, of 
our great concern and for the liberal interpretation of the circumstances coupled with 
our mode of proceeding. Little...can be conceived of the immense difficulties we have 
had to go through. My journal...is nothing but a series of the most terrific occur- 
rences, it makes one shudder...to face such attacks as those we have so frequently re- 
pelled. Our miners considered our shield such a protection that they never flinched 
from their post, while the less intelligent class, the laborers, were frequently panic- 
struck and running away. When we come to publish those occurrences...those who 
have lately raised their voice and those who have been so inconsiderate as to coun- 
tenance new theories will regret having suffered themselves to be so deluded. A tun- 
nel, it is true, has been attempted before and the work done was extended to 1040 
feet, but what was done was only a drain for the projected tunnel. It was what is 
technically called a driftway executed by the best miners of the country. The size 
was two feet six inches in width at top, thirty-six inches at bottom, and five feet high. 
Our tunnel...is 38 feet wide and 22 feet high...sixty times larger....The first was Jost at 
the first attack of the river, whereas we have ease our Siig: twice and are now as 
brilliant as ever.. fe de. 16-63

Dear Ellen 


Thank you for your letter and the two BRUNEL xeroxes. 


The Letter of 1805 is unquestionably from Marc Isambard 
Brunel. At this time he was living in Portsea and 
superintending the erection of the block-making machinery 
at Portsmouth Dockyard which was being manufactured by 
Maudslay to whom the letter is addressed. 


I know he sometimes signs himself 'M.I. Brunel' but he 
does also sign himself ‘I. Brunel', more particularly 
as he was always known as Isambard. I enclose the 
first page of a paper given by Lady Gladwyn who is a 
direct descendent and guardian of all Brunel family 
matters! 


As for the second letter, it certainly is not from M.I 
Brunel who was dead by then and the writing bears no 
resemblence to I.K.'s hand (by this stage in his life 
practically impossible to read). Brunel is a Normandy 
name (there are still Brunels in the Caen telephone 
directory!) and, as you know, the part of France where 
Marc Isambard came from. | 


I fear, therefore, that the second letter is a red herring 
as far as the engineers are concerned, I enclose, for 
purposes of Chr On a xerox of a letter by I.K. which 

I have. Although it is dated a few years later than the 
one in question, I think you will see that they are not 
at all in the same hand. 


I hope all this is of some help. Let me know if there is 
anything else I can do. 


In the meantime best wishes and I hope to see you either 
here or in Washington.

BRUNEL,, Sir Marc Isambard 
ALS to M. Briouét, SP July 1620 


An important letter in which Brunel describes his present difficulties resulting from 
rumour and gossip which have prevented him from getting suitable work for some 
time in several countries. For this reason he is especially enthusiastic in his 
hopes that his plans for the construction of a bridge at Rouen should be accepted. 
He describes various aspects of his work in great detail, referring to the way in 
which he reorganized the system for handling timber while he was working on the 
sawmills and dockyard at Chatham. He also discusses at some length the relative 
merits of stone and wood in the construction of bridges. 


Not long after this letter, in 1821, Brunel found himself in serious financial 
difficulties and was put in prison for debt. 


Brunel opens the letter by thanking Briguet for presenting his scheme for the bridge 

at Rouen to the appropriate authorities, and states ''’...I was very anxious that it 
should be welcomed by the administration of that town and to be able to carry out 

a plan which on the whole has enjoydd great favour here...''He feels however that 

the time for enterprises of this kind seems to have passed owing to the amount of 
previous unsuccessful speculation on bridges, canals and reservoirs. "... The 
public has been duped by the speculators, who come forward onlyto get out at the 
earliest opportunity, as usual..."He cites the depressing example of two bridges 
which were expected to be good enterprises: one cost more thah Sb2060ckookbh000« 
skonhing £1200, 000 to £1400, 000 sterling xxx and does not yield more than £12000 

a year; the other, the greatly admired iron bridge, despite being in the centre of 

the city, is not paying its way, and what is worse is beginning to prove that metal 

is the worst material to work in for bridges. "'...That has always been my opinion 
and it is because of this view that I have been working on and am at present occupied with 
a new system of construction involving a combination. ..designed to achieve strength 
without an excess of material..." Brunel expounds his views on the suitability of 
various materials at some length, declaring that stone is in every way a preferable 
material to work in, although it is more expensive. He refers to a suspension bridge 
now under construction, being made of wood for the floor and soft iron for the chains - 
this will be economic but not as strong as the one he has in mind. 


Brunel then gives in some detail the background to his recent fall from favour, which 
is resulting in his lack of work and his present desire to be given the contract for 
the Rouen bridge: 

'...l am very anxious to have something to do as I have had no work since my last 
travels from Paris. Ifin part it was this trip which did me harm, on the other hand 
it was M. Dupin who administered the final blow. Just for the sake of sounding 
entertaining he has compromised me with the Archdukes of Austria and the Emperor 


Alexandre. Itxwasxsettingmmexmpoomsxaxbmexmeexnx&x It would set me up as a braggart 

to gp make public dealings unworthy of these personnages; for they could only make 

me such offers in confidence and if they had done so, although it was an honour, I 
could not have availed myself of them in such a manner, and to the best of my knowledge 
what Mr Dupin claims is false. All that remained was to say to the Admiralty Minister 
in England and to the whole of England thatMr B. was twice refused by France. So 

why pay an individual who under the pretext of and a piece of English speculation | 
is going to offer his services, without explaining more than one refushl. What is the 
result? I have lost all hope of being employed and I cannot receive my past pay. I 
used to receive almost £1600 a year and now I have nothing..." Owing to Dupin's 
jealousy and his misinformed remarks Brunel feels he has little choice now but 

to leave the country and seek employment elsewhere. 


Dupin kax in addition criticized Brunel's plans for Chatham before the work was 
finished and thereby jeopardized some of his schemes. Brunel describes his new 
methods of excavation prior to building at Chatham, his plans for the treatment of 

oak timbers in the same way as resinous woods, and his careful selection of particular 
timbers for particular parts of the building. Owing to Dupin's criticism, he spent 
three years trying to press his schemes and received constant setbacks. Moreover 
Dupin had the audacity to claim that Brxmex he had lent him the plans for Chatham. 

Now Brunel is constantly having to go from department to department solliciting 

money due to him. Despite his petitioning and the support of the Duke of York and 

the Duke of Wellington, he feels he will have little success. 


Dupin is apparently now trying to mend the damage he has done to Brunel's career, but 
with little success. Brunel claims that Dupin has little skill, especially as far as 
mechanics are concerned and is at-heart only a theorist. 


He ends the letter ruefully comparing his former position of influence with the sorry — 
present position. Before he made major decisions at Tribunals and was compared to 
Newton, now he will have to struggle to get work as a result of it being known that he 
refused the Archdukes and the Emperor, and thus compromised them.
